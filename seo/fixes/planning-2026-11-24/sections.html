Do you need planning permission for a log cabin holiday let?

Usually yes. Permitted development for outbuildings in England only covers a building required for a purpose incidental to the enjoyment of your house [1]. The Planning Portal says these rules do not cover any other change of use of the land or outbuilding, or use as separate self-contained living accommodation [2]. Government technical guidance adds that an incidental purpose does not cover separate self-contained accommodation or primary living accommodation such as a bedroom, bathroom or kitchen [3].

Letting a cabin to paying guests is not a use incidental to your own home, so plan on applying for planning permission, whether the cabin is in your garden or on other land. GOV.UK guidance for holiday-let owners says your local planning authority decides whether you need planning permission, and tells you to contact your council to confirm [5].

England has no planning use class for short-term lets. The government consulted in 2023 on a new class, “C5 Short Term Let” [4]. GOV.UK still shows the outcome as “We are analysing your feedback”, and the Use Classes Order on legislation.gov.uk contains classes C5 and C6 for Wales only [4]. A mandatory national registration scheme for short-term lets in England is not yet in force; GOV.UK says it is expected to begin in March 2027 [5].

A cabin with sleeping accommodation is also not exempt from building regulations [6]. Check with your local planning authority before you order.

Planning a holiday site? See our cabins for holiday sites or our trade partner page. Whether any model needs planning permission depends on your site and its use, so ask your local planning authority first.

Holiday log cabins in an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty or National Park

Expect a full planning application, with stricter tests. Holiday cabins are not permitted development anywhere (see above), and in protected areas even garden outbuildings face tighter limits: in National Parks, the Broads, Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (now called National Landscapes) and World Heritage Sites, buildings more than 20 m from the house may cover no more than 10 m² in total [1] [2].

The National Planning Policy Framework published on 17 August 2026 replaces the December 2024 version [8]. Its policy N4 on Protected Landscapes (National Parks, the Broads and National Landscapes) says development there should be limited in scale and extent, and sensitively located and designed, and that substantial weight should be placed on conserving and enhancing the natural beauty of these areas. Major development should be refused other than in exceptional circumstances, and development in the setting of a Protected Landscape should also be sensitively located and designed [7]. Many National Park authorities and councils also publish their own tourism and holiday-accommodation policies.

Ask your local planning authority, or the National Park authority, for pre-application advice before you buy.

Do you need planning permission for two log cabins as holiday lets in Scotland?

Expect to need it. Scottish Government guidance on short-term lets says planning permission is required for all “development” [9], and the planning Act defines development as building and other operations on land or any material change in the use of buildings or land [10]. Householder permitted development (Class 3A) only covers a building for a purpose incidental to enjoying your house, and not one that is a dwelling [16] [18].

Three other rules apply:

  • Licence. The short-term let licensing scheme is mandatory for all short-term let accommodation in Scotland, including holiday cottages and unconventional accommodation such as pods and yurts, however long you let for. Since the law changed on 1 October 2022, new hosts must get a licence from the council before accepting bookings or receiving guests [12].
  • Control areas. In a short-term let control area, changing a house or flat to a short-term let is treated as a material change of use (with some exceptions) and so always needs planning permission [13]. The Scottish Government names Edinburgh (designated 5 September 2022) and the Badenoch and Strathspey ward of Highland (4 March 2024) as control areas [12]; ask your council whether others now apply.
  • Building warrant. This is a separate approval from planning, with its own exemptions (see the next section) [15] [19].

Contact your council’s planning and building standards teams first.

Do I need planning permission for a garden log cabin in Scotland?

Often not, if it meets Scotland’s own permitted development rules (Class 3A). No part of the building may be higher than 4 m, any part within 1 m of your boundary may be no higher than 2.5 m, and the eaves may be no higher than 3 m. It must not be forward of a wall forming the principal or side elevation where that elevation fronts a road, and development may not cover more than half of the front or rear garden. In a conservation area the footprint is limited to 8 m², and in the grounds of a listed building to 4 m². The building must not be a dwelling [16] [18].

A building warrant is a separate question. A detached single-storey building ancillary to a house is exempt up to 8 m², unless it is within 1 m of the house (and not at least 1 m from every boundary), contains sleeping accommodation, or has a flue, a fixed combustion appliance or a sanitary facility [19]. From 8 m² to 30 m², such a building does not need a warrant but must still meet the building standards, unless it is within 1 m of the house (and not at least 1 m from every boundary) or has a fixed combustion appliance or a sanitary facility [15]. Above 30 m², or outside these conditions, check with building standards.

The same rules apply in Aberdeenshire; check with your council.

Can you live in a log cabin as a permanent residence?

Not under permitted development. In England, the outbuilding rules do not cover use as separate self-contained living accommodation [2], and an incidental purpose does not include primary living accommodation such as a bedroom, bathroom or kitchen [3]. In Scotland, Class 3A permitted development does not cover a building that is a dwelling [16] [18].

A home also falls outside the building regulations exemption for small detached buildings, which only covers buildings with no sleeping accommodation [6]. Expect to need full building regulations approval (a building warrant in Scotland) as well as planning permission.

Some holiday lodges fall within the legal definition of a caravan rather than a building: a structure designed or adapted for human habitation that can be moved from one place to another. A twin-unit structure assembled on site still counts if, when assembled, it is no more than 20 m long, 6.8 m wide and 3.05 m high inside [20]. That changes which rules apply. Ask your local planning authority before you plan to live in a cabin.

Do you need planning permission for a log cabin on agricultural land?

Yes, for living or holiday use. Agricultural permitted development in England (Part 6, Class A of the General Permitted Development Order) covers buildings reasonably necessary for agriculture on a unit of 5 hectares or more. It does not cover the erection of a dwelling, or a building not designed for agricultural purposes [21]. If a lodge meets the legal definition of a caravan, different rules apply (see above) [20].

In Scotland, using land for agriculture is not development, but building operations and material changes of use are [10]. Ask your local planning authority for pre-application advice.

Log cabin building regulations in the UK

Building regulations are separate from planning permission. In England, a detached single-storey building with a floor area up to 15 m² and no sleeping accommodation is exempt; up to 30 m² it is exempt if it has no sleeping accommodation and is at least 1 m from the boundary or built substantially of non-combustible material [6]. A cabin for sleeping, a holiday let or a home is not covered by that exemption [6].

Wales: the same Schedule 2 Class VI exemption is shown on legislation.gov.uk as extending to England and Wales [6] [23].

Northern Ireland: a detached single-storey building up to 30 m² with no sleeping accommodation is exempt if it is built substantially of non-combustible material or is at no point less than 1 m from the nearest dwelling, road or boundary; up to 15 m² it is exempt if it has no sleeping accommodation and is at least 1 m from a dwelling [24].

Scotland: the approval is a building warrant, with its own exemptions (see the Scotland section) [15] [19].

Electrical work in an outbuilding must also meet the building regulations (see above). Check with your local building control or building standards team.

Wales and Northern Ireland in brief

Wales. Outbuilding rules are close to England’s, but not identical. In Wales the 4 m height limit applies to a building with a roof with more than one pitch (3 m otherwise), the 2.5 m limit applies within 2 m of the boundary, eaves are limited to 2.5 m, and any part within 2 m of the house may be no higher than 1.5 m [23]. Since 20 October 2022, Wales has separate use classes for main homes (C3), homes not used as a sole or main residence (C5) and commercial short-term lets (C6) [25]. Changes between these classes are permitted development, and the same 2022 order sets the procedure councils use for Article 4 directions that withdraw permitted development rights [25]. Ask your council whether a direction applies in your area.

Northern Ireland. Permitted development is set by the Planning (General Permitted Development) Order (Northern Ireland) 2015. A garden building must be for a purpose incidental to the house, no higher than 4 m, with eaves no higher than 2.5 m within 2 m of the boundary, not forward of a wall facing a road, and not for use as a dwelling; in a World Heritage Site, National Park or AONB, buildings more than 20 m from the house may cover no more than 10 m² [27]. Your council’s planning office confirms what applies.
